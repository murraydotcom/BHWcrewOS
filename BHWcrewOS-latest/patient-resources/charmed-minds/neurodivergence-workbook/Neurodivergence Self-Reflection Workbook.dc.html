PLEASE READ FIRST
These forms are for reflection and conversation. They do not diagnose a condition, generate a score, replace individualized care, or provide crisis assessment. If you may be in immediate danger, tell your clinician or contact emergency support in your area now.

How to Use This Workbook

  • Start with Form 01 so therapy can be made more accessible.
  • Choose only the forms connected to your goals. You do not need to finish the workbook in order.
  • Your answer can be 'not sure,' 'it depends,' or 'I do not want to answer.'
  • A support person may help write your words, but your preferences and goals should remain central.
  • Bring one page to a session, or ask the clinician to complete it with you.
  • Review Form 16 every 4-6 sessions and change the therapy plan when it is not useful or accessible.
IMPORTANT
A reflection form cannot determine whether a behavior is a harmless stim, a sign of overload, a medical problem, self-injury, or suicidal behavior. Tell a qualified clinician about injuries, thoughts of dying, plans to harm yourself or someone else, abuse, or inability to stay safe.

Additional Education and Reflection Pages

These optional pages add education and questions from the expanded source library. Choose only what fits your goals. You may answer by speaking, writing, typing, drawing, choosing options, using AAC, or asking a trusted person to help record your words. These pages do not diagnose a condition or replace medical, nutrition, substance-use, occupational-therapy, trauma, or crisis care.

FORM 17 - Brain-Friendly Time and Task Support

Executive-function difficulty can create a gap between knowing what to do and being able to do it at the right time. This is not proof that you are lazy, careless, unintelligent, or unmotivated. A useful support makes the next action easier to notice, start, and finish.

Strategies to try
  • Put the task on a calendar at a specific time instead of leaving it only on a to-do list.
  • Estimate preparation, searching, breaks, transitions, and cleanup—not just working time.
  • Make the first step very small and place the materials where you will use them.
  • Try a visible timer, transition alarm, body double, written checklist, or reduced-distraction space.
Questions to reflect on
  • Where does this task get stuck: remembering, starting, sequencing, staying with it, switching, or finishing?
  • What needs to be outside my head and visible?
  • What is the smallest action that would count as starting?
  • What support will I test, and how will I know whether it helped?

FORM 18 - Mindful Attention without Perfect Focus

Mindfulness does not require an empty mind. The practice can be noticing where your attention went and choosing how to return. It may be short, moving, eyes-open, sensory-based, or part of an everyday activity. Stop or change the practice if it increases panic, dissociation, pain, shame, or overload.

Strategies to try
  • Choose one anchor: sound, sight, touch, feet on the floor, an object, walking, or breath.
  • Practice for a repeatable amount of time—even thirty seconds—and count returns rather than judging distractions.
  • Try STOP: stop, take a workable pause, observe what is happening inside and around you, then proceed by choice.
Questions to reflect on
  • Which anchor feels easiest or safest today?
  • Would movement, open eyes, music, or a shorter practice help?
  • How did I talk to myself when my attention wandered?
  • Did this practice give me more choice, or did it make me feel trapped or flooded?

FORM 19 - Body Rhythm and Life-Stage Pattern Tracker

Sleep, pain, illness, stress, nourishment, medication, menstrual cycles, pregnancy, postpartum changes, and perimenopause may affect attention, energy, and mood. A pattern is useful information, but it does not prove a cause. Bring repeated or concerning patterns to a qualified clinician.

What I notice
  • Attention, memory, task initiation, restlessness, impulsivity, or emotional intensity
  • Sleep, pain, appetite, sensory sensitivity, energy, mood, and medication experience
  • Cycle or life-stage context, illness, stress, workload, and major routine changes
Questions to reflect on
  • What repeats, and what does not?
  • What else was happening at the same time?
  • What support helped during a harder period?
  • What pattern or question do I want to bring to my prescriber or medical clinician?

FORM 20 - Family or Caregiver Support and Autonomy Agreement

Support can be necessary without becoming surveillance or control. The goal is to match help to current skills and safety, make roles clear, protect privacy, and transfer responsibility gradually when the person is ready.

Questions to reflect on
  • Which tasks do I want to manage independently?
  • Where do I want reminders, teaching, shared work, backup, or full support?
  • What information may a caregiver receive, and what stays private?
  • What would trigger more support for safety, and what would show readiness for less support?
  • How will we talk about a missed task without shaming, lecturing, or taking over?
One agreement to test
Person responsible; cue or reminder; definition of done; backup plan; check-in time; repair if missed.

FORM 21 - Relationship and Shared-System Conversation

ADHD and other neurodivergent differences can affect time, follow-through, emotional reactions, sensory needs, and invisible labor. Explanation is not an excuse, and accountability does not require shame. A sustainable agreement fits both people's real capacity and includes a repair plan.

Questions to reflect on
  • What cycle keeps repeating between us?
  • What is each person assuming, feeling, protecting, and doing?
  • Which visible and invisible tasks are not shared fairly?
  • What agreement can both people realistically keep?
  • Who owns the task, what is the cue, when is it due, and what counts as done?
  • How will we repair a missed agreement without creating a parent-child dynamic?

FORM 22 - Adult Intimacy, Consent, and Connection

This optional adult page is about mutual consent and connection, not meeting a frequency target. Desire differences are common. Time, fatigue, resentment, workload, sensory comfort, trauma, pain, medication, privacy, and transitions can affect intimacy. Either person may decline, pause, or renegotiate at any time.

Questions to reflect on
  • What helps me feel safe, respected, connected, and present?
  • What sensory experiences are welcome, unwelcome, or variable?
  • How do I prefer to initiate, be invited, say yes, say no, or ask for a pause?
  • Are time, workload, conflict, or transition difficulties reducing connection before intimacy even begins?
  • What conversation, medical support, trauma support, or relationship repair may be needed first?

FORM 23 - Food, Body Signals, and Gentle Nourishment

Neurodivergence can affect remembering to eat, planning food, tolerating textures, noticing body signals, shopping, cooking, and eating during medication changes or stress. Hunger may show up as fatigue, irritability, nausea, food thoughts, restlessness, indecision, or loss of focus. Nourishment does not have to be perfect to be useful.

Strategies to try
  • Use predictable eating prompts if body signals arrive late or are hard to read.
  • Keep realistic preferred foods available for low-capacity days.
  • Add variety or nutrition to safe foods when welcome; do not remove safe foods as punishment.
  • Ask a registered dietitian for help when nutrition, medical conditions, or eating-disorder recovery require individualized care.
Questions to reflect on
  • What are my early, middle, and late signs that I may need food or fluids?
  • Which foods are safe, satisfying, affordable, culturally meaningful, and realistic?
  • Which executive-function or sensory barriers make eating harder?
  • What is one supportive addition—not a restriction—I want to test?

FORM 24 - Emotional Eating and Coping Map

Food can provide comfort, stimulation, numbness, transition, pleasure, or connection. That does not make you bad. The goal is to understand what eating does for you and add choices without shame. Restricting food may increase preoccupation, rebound eating, or binge patterns.

Questions to reflect on
  • What was happening before the urge or eating began?
  • Had I eaten enough and regularly earlier in the day?
  • What emotion, body state, sensory need, thought, place, person, or event was present?
  • What did eating provide in the short term?
  • What happened afterward, including any shame or restriction?
  • What additional support could meet part of the same need while food remains available?
WHEN TO SEEK MORE SUPPORT
Seek eating-disorder-informed and medical support for restriction, bingeing, purging, fainting, rapid changes, severe gastrointestinal symptoms, or feeling unable to eat safely.

FORM 25 - Alcohol, Coping, and Change Reflection

This optional adult page is nonjudgmental. People may choose abstinence, reduction, safer-use planning, or help deciding. Alcohol may be connected to stress, sleep, social pressure, sensory discomfort, trauma, attention, or habit. If you drink heavily or regularly, do not stop suddenly without medical advice; alcohol withdrawal can be dangerous.

Questions to reflect on
  • What does alcohol help with in the short term?
  • What does it cost me later—physically, emotionally, financially, relationally, sexually, or at work or school?
  • Which times, places, emotions, people, or routines are strong cues?
  • What change, if any, matters to me right now?
  • How important is that change, and how confident do I feel?
  • What medical, therapy, peer, transportation, or relationship support would make change safer?
GET URGENT MEDICAL HELP FOR
Severe shaking, seizures, hallucinations, confusion, chest pain, trouble breathing, inability to stay awake, or other emergency symptoms.

FORM 26 - Child or Teen Co-Regulation and School Support

A child's behavior may communicate overload, anxiety, sensory distress, unclear expectations, a lagging skill, low trust, or a need for connection. Regulation and access come before complex problem-solving. The goal is safety, communication, participation, and growing autonomy—not forced eye contact, stillness, disclosure, or harmless-trait suppression.

Questions for the young person and caregivers
  • What signs show that stress is building before a crisis?
  • What sounds, lights, touch, movement, social demands, transitions, or uncertainty make the situation harder?
  • What helps: fewer words, written choices, a safe adult, quiet space, movement, predictable routine, food, water, or more time?
  • What can adults change in the environment before asking the young person to cope harder?
  • What school accommodation, gradual plan, or communication method should be requested?
  • How will adults reconnect and repair after a hard moment?

FORM 27 - Sensory and Movement Support Experiment

Sensory and movement needs are individual and can change by situation. A strategy that calms one person may activate or hurt another. Try one safe option at a time, with consent, and observe the effect. Ask an occupational therapist or medical clinician when pain, falls, feeding, swallowing, toileting, motor, or significant daily-function concerns are present.

Questions to reflect on
  • Am I seeking more input, avoiding input, or both?
  • What does my body need: movement, pressure, quiet, light change, sound change, oral input, temperature change, or rest?
  • What strategy will I test, in what situation, and for how long?
  • Did it regulate, energize, distract, hurt, overwhelm, or have no effect?
  • What should I keep, change, or stop?

FORM 28 - Trauma-Therapy Readiness and Resources

Trauma processing—including EMDR—should be guided by a properly trained clinician. This page is for preparation and communication, not for processing traumatic memories alone. You remain allowed to pause, stop, decline a method, or return to stabilization.

Questions to reflect on with a clinician
  • What helps me feel oriented to the present and connected to my body or surroundings?
  • What are my early signs of shutdown, dissociation, panic, overload, or loss of speech?
  • What stop signal or communication method will we use?
  • What sensory, movement, play, drawing, story, or caregiver support makes therapy accessible?
  • How will we close the session and support the time afterward?
  • What would tell us that more preparation, consultation, or another treatment is needed first?

FORM 29 - Assessment and Referral Preparation

A screener or traits list can suggest questions, but it does not diagnose autism, ADHD, trauma, an eating disorder, or another condition. A full evaluation considers development, context, strengths, impairment, co-occurring conditions, medical and sleep factors, and information across settings.

Questions to prepare
  • What do I hope an assessment will help me understand or access?
  • What patterns were present in childhood, and what records or memories exist?
  • Where do difficulties show up now, and where do interest, structure, masking, or support make them less visible?
  • What strengths, sensory or communication needs, identities, and accommodations should the evaluator understand?
  • What anxiety, depression, trauma, sleep, substance, learning, medical, pain, or eating concerns also need consideration?
  • How would I want results explained, and who may receive them?

If an assessment is not available or does not confirm the expected diagnosis, your lived difficulties and access needs still deserve thoughtful support.
